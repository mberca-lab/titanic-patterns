The rule
in the data.What a model learns when it is trained on a historical decision.

In 1912 a human rule, women and children first, shaped who reached a lifeboat. It was applied unevenly, by class and by side of the ship. Train a model on that record and it learns the rule, not anyone’s worth. The same thing happens when a model learns from past promotions, hires or ratings.

NOT A SURVIVAL GAME

In memory of the about 1,500 people who died when Titanic sank on 15 April 1912.

This study reads the records of passengers; crew are not in the source. It names no one and scores no one.

Why this matters for people analytics

The label is a record of a decision.

In HR data the outcome is rarely a fact about a person. “Promoted”, “hired” and “high performer” record past decisions, made under a policy, by people, and not always evenly. A model trained on those labels learns the policy that produced them.

The Titanic is a stark, well-documented case of a decision rule written into an outcome. This page uses it to show how to recognise, measure and audit that inheritance, then repeats the audit on synthetic promotion data where the truth is known. The findings remain specific to this incomplete historical sample. It is not a model of employee behaviour or guidance for workplace decisions.

04 / Audit it like an HR system

Calibrated for everyone. Fair to no one?

Treat the reference model as if it were a selection tool: anyone with a predicted probability of 0.5 or more is “selected”. Then run the checks an HR analytics team would run, on out-of-fold mean probabilities. Cells with a denominator below 20 are suppressed.

Impact ratio for men at 0.5 (95% CI ). In the record itself it was : the threshold sharpened the rule.
Calibration ratio, women / men: observed rate divided by mean prediction. Both groups are well calibrated.
False-positive rate, women / men. Calibration and equal error rates cannot both hold when base rates differ this much.
  • Model at 0.5

The four-fifths rule of thumb. US federal selection guidelines (1978) treat a selection rate below 80% of the highest group’s as evidence of possible adverse impact. Report the ratio with an interval, not as a pass/fail light. Cited as context for analysts, not legal advice.
The impossibility. When base rates differ, a model cannot be calibrated within groups and have equal false-positive and false-negative rates (Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan, 2016; Chouldechova, 2017). An audit has to say which property it prioritises, and why.

Checklist, not legal advice

An audit memo in eight lines

  1. Ask what decision produced the label.Who decided, under which policy, and did it vary by manager, site or period?
  2. Describe before you predict.Outcome rates by group, with denominators and intervals.
  3. Check who is missing.Missing data that falls unevenly is a fairness finding in its own right.
  4. Test proxies directly.Can the other columns predict the protected attribute? Report that AUC.
  5. Don’t remove the attribute and declare victory.Measure how much of the gap a blind model still reproduces.
  6. Report impact ratios with intervals.Against the 0.8 rule of thumb, and for the record as well as the model.
  7. Check calibration and error rates per group.Say which one you prioritise when they conflict.
  8. Suppress small cells.Here, any rate with a denominator below 20 is not shown.

    What this cannot tell us

    • Passengers only. . .
    • Recorded sex is binary in the source. These are recorded categories, not identities.
    • Ages are missing for of passengers, unevenly by class.
    • Associations, not causes. Nothing here explains why any individual lived or died.
    • Synthetic means synthetic. The promotion data shows mechanics, not any real organisation.
    • Context, not legal advice. The four-fifths rule and impossibility results frame the audit; they do not settle it.
